Pool projects carry more risk than most hardscape work — more trades involved, more that's hidden until construction starts, and a much bigger check being written. A few patterns show up often enough in pool bids that they're worth checking for specifically.

## "Demo" or "prep" as a single vague line

Pool deck removal, old equipment removal, and site prep are frequently lumped into one line with no detail. If you're doing a remodel, ask what's actually included — full demo and haul-off, or just the visible surface? What happens if something's found during demo that wasn't anticipated?

## No mention of what happens if something's found during demo

It's normal for a pool remodel bid to not know everything until the old decking or plaster comes off — cracked plumbing, sub-base issues, and structural surprises happen. What's not normal is a bid with zero language about how that's handled. A stated contingency process (not necessarily a number, but a process) is what separates a bid that's planned for reality from one that's setting up a renegotiation later.

## Drainage left unaddressed

Deck slope, drain placement, and how water moves away from the pool and the house are structural decisions, not finishing touches — and they're commonly left out of a bid entirely. If drainage isn't mentioned, ask directly how it's being handled.

## Coping and tile replacement without a match plan

If you're only replacing part of the pool's coping or tile, ask how the contractor plans to match the existing material — discontinued tile lines are common, and "we'll match it" without a sourcing plan is a preview of a disagreement later.

## Equipment allowances with no model specified

Pool equipment (pumps, filters, heaters) varies widely in price and efficiency. An allowance with no equipment model or efficiency rating attached makes it easy to spec something cheaper than what you assumed you were paying for.

## Pricing that's dramatically lower than every other bid you got

The most expensive bid isn't automatically the best, but a bid that's far below everyone else's for the same described scope is worth extra scrutiny — it usually means either a scope gap (something's not actually included that you assumed was) or a corner that's going to get cut somewhere you can't see yet.

None of these red flags mean a contractor is acting in bad faith — a lot of pool bids are written informally, and gaps are often just gaps, not deception. But on a project this size, it's worth having someone who builds this kind of work read the bid specifically and tell you what's missing before you sign.
